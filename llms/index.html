Phase 1 · Pretraining

Read the whole internet.
Fill in the blanks.

Self-supervised: the text is its own teacher. No labels.

The cat sat on the ___ → mat

Result: a text completer, not an assistant.

Self-supervised learning: hide the next word, make the model guess it, check against the real text. Every sentence on the internet becomes free training data — no human labeling needed.

Phase 2 · Instruction tuning (SFT)

Learns to answer.

What's the capital of France?Paris.
Translate "good morning" to French.Bonjour.
Summarize this email in one line.The meeting moved to Monday.
Write a function that adds two numbers.def add(a, b): return a + b

Thousands of pairs, written by humans.

Before SFT, ask "What's the capital of France?" and the base model may just continue with more questions: "What's the capital of Germany?" SFT teaches it the question-then-answer format.

Generation

One token at a time.

What's the capital of France?

The capital of France is Paris . <end>

Predict → sample → append → repeat.

Each new token is predicted from everything before it, sampled, appended, and fed back in. The loop stops at a special end token.

Phase 3 · RLHF

Learns which answer is better.

Explain gravity to a 5-year-old."The curvature of spacetime.""An invisible pull that makes things fall." 👍
Is this mushroom safe to eat?"Yes, looks fine.""Can't tell from a photo. Ask an expert." 👍
Write a haiku about coffee."Dark cup, morning steam / ..." 👍"Coffee is a drink."

Humans pick. A reward model learns their taste. The LLM chases the reward.

Humans see two answers to the same prompt and pick the better one. Those choices train a reward model, and the LLM is then optimized to score high on it: clearer, safer, more helpful.

Phase 3 · RLHF

Who decides "better"?

Which party should I vote for?"Vote for party X.""Here's where each party stands." 👍
Is God real?"No.""People hold different beliefs. Here are the main views." 👍

The company. And its business.

On politics and religion, "better" is not a fact — it's a choice. The lab writes the rules raters follow. An answer that offends half your customers is bad for business, so the model learns to be neutral, careful, brand-safe. RLHF encodes the company's interests.

Part 2

Controversial

My takes.

Talking about LLMs is hard these days.

Pros and cons used to be trivial to discuss.

Why? Bad marketing of every previous AI generation.

People only noticed them after LLMs. Yet each was big progress.

Back in the day, talking about the pros and cons of a technology was trivial. Now criticizing LLMs feels hard. One reason: earlier AI generations (vision, speech, recommendation, game-playing RL) were badly marketed. People only understood them after the LLM wave — even though each of them was huge progress.

Question 1

Are LLMs getting better?

According to who?

Sam Altman, Dario Amodei: "Yes, definitely."

Me: Yes and no. Not significantly.

Diminishing returns: each step costs more, gives less.

We have a history of this.

Expert systems1980s · boom, then winter
→
CNNs2012 · deep learning sees
→
Transformers2017 · today's LLMs
My take: yes and no — not significantly. The law of diminishing returns applies. Each jump needs far more compute, data and money for a smaller gain. And we have a history of this: AI has gone in waves. Expert systems were the big thing in the 1980s, hit their limits, and the money dried up: an AI winter. Then CNNs, around 2012, made deep learning work for images. Then transformers, in 2017, gave us today's LLMs. Each wave flattened like this curve, and the next jump came from a new idea, not from more of the old one.

I say this so if LLMs fail, you don't lose belief in AI. Just in LLMs.

The first four echo Yann LeCun's list of what LLMs lack: understanding the physical world, persistent memory, reasoning, planning. When the model fails, the usual answer is "skill issue" (you prompted it wrong) or "training issue" (the next version will fix it). Why I'm saying all this: so that if the current LLMs fail, you don't lose faith in AI — only in LLMs.

What I believe

My belief: let's start optimizing ourselves around what already exists, and stop worrying about the next big thing — because there probably isn't one. We still have a great edge.
